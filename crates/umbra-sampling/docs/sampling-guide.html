Umbra Sampling Guide

A renderer can't add up all the light in a scene exactly. It guesses, many times, at random spots, and averages the guesses. umbra-sampling chooses those spots. It makes numbers that look random but can be reproduced exactly, spreads them evenly, and bends them into the shapes the renderer needs: points on a lens, directions above a surface, wavelengths of light. This guide shows each step in pictures first and formulas second. It assumes only school maths.

Part I · Theory

How a renderer chooses its numbers

Rendering means measuring light that arrives from everywhere at once. The measurement can only be estimated, by sampling. This part explains why sampling works, where the numbers come from, and how they are bent into useful shapes.

The maths toolkit, in pictures

In plain wordsThis guide needs four ideas: numbers spread evenly between 0 and 1, a curve that says where values crowd, whole numbers seen as rows of bits, and how stretching space thins points out. Each one has a picture below. Arrows, angles, areas under curves and averages are drawn in the Camera Guide's toolkit. How computers store decimals is in the Math Guide.

Uniform numbers in [0, 1)

Every sampling routine starts from numbers u between 0 and 1. Uniform means each stretch of the ruler gets numbers in proportion to its length. The bracket “[0, 1)” means 0 can happen and 1 can't. In f32, the largest number below 1 is ONE_MINUS_EPSILON = 0.99999994, and the crate clamps to it.

chance that a ≤ u < b = b − a

Density and running total

A density p(x), or pdf, says where values crowd: tall means often. The area under it between two values is the chance of landing between them, and the whole area is 1. Its running total P(x), the CDF, is the shaded area up to x. It climbs from 0 to 1, steeply where p is tall.

P(x) = ∫ p from the start up to x   P(end) = 1

Whole numbers as bits

A u64 is a row of 64 switches, written in hex (base 16, 4 bits per digit: 0x1f = 31). XOR (⊕) flips the bits of a where b has a 1. Shift (>>) slides bits right and drops the ones that fall off. Multiplication wraps around like a clock: only the last 64 bits are kept. Hashes and PCG32 are built from these three moves.

a ⊕ b   a >> k = ⌊a ⁄ 2k⌋   a·b mod 2⁶⁴

Stretching space thins points out

A warp moves every point of a square somewhere else. Where it stretches a patch to 4 times its area, the same points spread over 4 times the room, so the density there drops to a quarter. The stretch factor is called the Jacobian, |J|. Every pdf in this crate is the input density divided by it.

pout = pin ⁄ |J|,   |J| = area after ⁄ area before

Why sample: Monte Carlo

In plain wordsA pixel's colour is the total of light arriving through every point of the pixel, from every direction, at every wavelength. That total is an area under a curve with no formula. Monte Carlo guesses it: measure the curve at random spots, divide each measurement by how likely its spot was, and average. One guess is noisy. The average of many homes in on the true value. Everything in this crate makes those spots: reproducibly, evenly spread, and shaped like the thing being measured.

The general recipe is the Monte Carlo estimator. Draw N random spots Xi with density p. Divide each measurement f(Xi) by how likely its spot was, and average. On average this gives exactly the right answer, whatever p is, as long as p is above zero wherever f is. That property is called being unbiased.

The noise is the problem. There are two ways to lower it without paying for more samples, and the rest of Part I is about both:

  • Spread the spots evenly instead of letting them clump by chance. That is stratification and low discrepancy: section 4 and the Halton sampler.
  • Put the spots where f is big, then divide by p to stay fair. That is importance sampling. It needs a way to produce spots with a chosen density: the inversion method and the warps of sections 6 to 13. If p has exactly the shape of f, every f ⁄ p is the same number and the noise disappears.

Every sampling function in the crate therefore comes in a pair: sample_… makes the spot, and …_pdf (or a pdf field) says how likely it was. Many also have an invert_… that goes back from the spot to the numbers that made it.

Hashing: random-looking, repeatable

In plain wordsA hash is a blender for numbers. Put in a few whole numbers, such as a pixel, a digit position and a seed, and get back 64 bits that look random. Change the input a tiny bit and about half the output bits change. The same input always gives the same output. That is exactly what a renderer wants: numbers that look random but can be made again, on any machine, at any time. Umbra uses pbrt-v4's two hashes, so a seed gives pbrt-v4's numbers.

mix_bits scrambles one 64-bit number: three rounds of “shift right and XOR”, with two multiplications by large odd constants in between. Each shift lets high bits stir the low ones. Each multiplication lets low bits stir the high ones. It is Stafford's “Mix13” finaliser, as in pbrt-v4:

pub fn mix_bits(mut v: u64) -> u64 {
    v ^= v >> 31;
    v = v.wrapping_mul(0x7fb5_d329_728e_a185);
    v ^= v >> 27;
    v = v.wrapping_mul(0x81da_def4_bc2d_d44d);
    v ^= v >> 33;
    v
}

murmur_hash_64a is Austin Appleby's MurmurHash2 for any list of bytes. It reads 8 bytes at a time as a little-endian u64, mixes each one in, then mixes in the leftover bytes and finishes with a last shuffle. hash feeds it the bytes of its arguments, one after the other with no padding, each in its C++ layout. The trait Hashable says how many bytes a type has and writes them. hash_float keeps the low 32 bits and scales them into [0, 1).

Where the crate uses them: Rng::new(i) starts its stream at offset mix_bits(i). Each digit permutation of the Halton sampler is chosen by hash((base, digit_index, seed)). Owen scrambling hashes each digit with mix_bits.

PCG32, the random number generator

In plain wordsPCG32 holds one 64-bit number, its state. Each time you ask for a number, it moves the state one step along a fixed loop that visits all 2⁶⁴ values, and hands you a scrambled 32-bit view of where it was. Different streams are different loops. The step is a simple formula, so the generator can also jump a million steps ahead at once, or tell how many steps apart two generators are.

A plain “multiply and add” generator (an LCG) has weak low bits: the lowest bit simply alternates. PCG fixes that with its output step, in pbrt-v4's form:

fn next_u32(&mut self) -> u32 {
    let old = self.state;
    self.state = old.wrapping_mul(PCG32_MULT).wrapping_add(self.inc);
    let (xorshifted, rot) = ((((old >> 18) ^ old) >> 27) as u32, (old >> 59) as u32);
    xorshifted.rotate_right(rot)
}

The top bits of the state are the best mixed, so they choose how far to rotate. rng.uniform::<f32>() turns the 32 bits into a number in [0, 1) by multiplying by 2⁻³². A u32 near 2³² rounds up to 1.0 when it becomes an f32, so the result is clamped to ONE_MINUS_EPSILON. uniform_below(b) gives a whole number below b, rejecting the few values that would make some results more likely than others.

Rng::new(i) picks stream i and starts it at offset mix_bits(i), as pbrt-v4's RNG(seqIndex) does. advance(n) applies the step n times by repeated squaring, in about log₂ n operations; a negative n goes back. &a - &b finds how many draws separate two generators on the same stream. Rng is Clone but deliberately not Copy, so a stream is never duplicated by accident.

Today the Halton sampler doesn't draw from Rng: its numbers come from radical inverses. The crate's statistical tests do, and so will the samplers still to come (section 19).

Spreading points evenly

In plain wordsRandom points clump and leave holes, just by chance. Low-discrepancy points are placed on purpose, so every region gets its fair share however you slice the square. The trick is the radical inverse: write the point's number in base 2, mirror its digits around the decimal point, and the result always falls into the biggest gap left so far. Base 2 for one dimension, base 3 for the next, base 5 after that: that is the Halton sequence. Scrambling shuffles the digits, so each seed gets its own version without losing the evenness.

Spreading points evenly has a name, stratification: cut the domain into equal cells and put one point in each. The radical inverse does better than a fixed grid. Its first 2k points in base 2 fill every cell of width 1⁄2k, for every k at once, so any number of samples is well spread. Pairing base 2 with base 3 does the same in 2D: the first 2j·3k points put one point in each of the 2j × 3k boxes. The measure of how uneven a point set is, the worst gap between “share of the points in a box” and “area of the box”, is its discrepancy. Hence the name.

pub fn radical_inverse(base_index: usize, mut a: u64) -> f32 {
    let (base, limit) = base_and_limit(base_index);      // base = PRIMES[base_index]
    let inv_base = 1.0 / float_u64(base);
    let mut inv_base_m = 1.0;
    let mut reversed_digits = 0_u64;
    while a != 0 && reversed_digits < limit {
        let next = a / base;
        let digit = a - next * base;                       // the lowest digit
        reversed_digits = reversed_digits * base + digit;   // pushed onto the mirror
        inv_base_m *= inv_base;
        a = next;
    }
    (float_u64(reversed_digits) * inv_base_m).min(ONE_MINUS_EPSILON)
}

The bases are the primes, so no two dimensions share a pattern. PRIMES holds the first 1000 of them, 2 to 7919, computed by trial division when the crate compiles. That gives 1000 dimensions, PRIME_TABLE_SIZE.

Plain Halton points have two flaws. Every render gets the same points. And large bases start badly: while a is below the base, the radical inverse is just a ⁄ b, so the first points of two large bases lie on a straight line. Scrambling fixes both by permuting the digits before mirroring them:

  • Random digit permutation, pbrt-v4's default. Each digit position of each base gets its own shuffle of the digits 0 to b − 1, chosen by a hash of the base, the position and the seed. Since a shuffle maps each digit to a different digit, each cell still gets exactly one point. The loop runs over every digit an f32 can still feel, n_digits, because a leading 0 digit may be shuffled into something else. scrambled_radical_inverse
  • Owen scrambling. The shuffle of each digit also depends on all the digits already mirrored, through mix_bits(hash ⊕ reversed_digits). That is more random and still stratified. pbrt-v4's Halton sampler seeds it from the dimension only, not the seed. owen_scrambled_radical_inverse

Both use permutation_element(i, l, p), Kensler's trick for the ith element of a random shuffle of 0 … l − 1 chosen by p, computed by hashing without storing the shuffle. inverse_radical_inverse undoes the mirror on whole digits; the sampler needs it in the next section.

The Halton sampler

In plain wordsA sampler gives each Pixel Sample its own list of numbers, one per dimension: the wavelengths take one, the time one, the lens two, and every bounce of the path a few more. The Halton sampler's list is one point of the Halton sequence. The clever part is which point. Its first two dimensions, base 2 and base 3, are treated as a position on the image. Each pixel gets the Halton points that land inside it, so the points of neighbouring pixels fit together evenly. The rest of that same point feeds everything else.

The Sampler trait is pbrt-v4's interface. start_pixel_sample(pixel, sample_index, dimension) resets the sampler for one Pixel Sample. After it, get_1d, get_2d and get_pixel_2d read the next numbers. What they return depends only on the pixel, the sample index and the dimension, never on earlier calls: a Pixel Sample is a pure function, so it can be computed again on any thread or Worker (ADR 0004, ADR 0022).

HaltonSampler::new works out the grid. The base scales are the smallest power of 2 at least the image width, and the smallest power of 3 at least the height, both capped at 128. Images larger than 128 × 128 reuse the same tile of strata. The product of the two scales is the stride: Halton points a, a + stride, a + 2·stride, … all fall in the same stratum. So a pixel's Pixel Samples are every stride-th point, starting from the first one in that pixel:

fn start_pixel_sample(&mut self, pixel: Point2i, sample_index: u32, dimension: u32) {
    let sample_stride = self.base_scales[0] * self.base_scales[1];
    self.halton_index = self.first_index(pixel) + u64::from(sample_index) * sample_stride;
    self.dimension = usize::try_from(dimension.max(2)).expect("a dimension fits usize");
}

fn get_pixel_2d(&mut self) -> Point2f {
    Point2f::new(
        radical_inverse(0, self.halton_index >> self.base_exponents[0]),
        radical_inverse(1, self.halton_index / self.base_scales[1]),
    )
}

Finding first_index is a small puzzle. The pixel's column fixes the lowest base-2 digits of the index, its row fixes the lowest base-3 digits, and inverse_radical_inverse reads those digits off. One whole number has to satisfy both. The Chinese remainder theorem builds it from two multiplicative inverses, precomputed with Euclid's algorithm in multiplicative_inverse. get_pixel_2d then drops the digits that chose the pixel: the rest is the position inside it.

The other dimensions start at 2 and use the configured randomisation, through HaltonRandomizer: plain, permuted digits, or Owen (section 4). Past the 1000th prime they wrap back to 2, as in pbrt-v4.

The inversion method

In plain wordsTo turn even numbers into numbers with a chosen density, use the running total backwards. Start at your number u on the vertical axis, go across to the CDF curve, then down to x. Where the density is tall, the CDF is steep, and a steep stretch of curve catches more of the u values. Every warp in this crate is this one idea, sometimes in several dimensions.

The recipe in three steps: find the CDF P by adding up the density; solve P(x) = u for x; feed it uniform u. The crate has three one-dimensional densities with a closed-form answer:

  • Linear on [0, 1], from a at 0 to b at 1. Solving P(x) = u is a quadratic equation. pbrt-v4 writes its root in a form that stays accurate when a ≈ b, where the textbook form would divide by almost zero. The bilinear and triangle warps build on it.
  • Exponential with rate a: how far light travels in fog before something happens, for example. x = −ln(1 − u) ⁄ a.
  • Visible wavelengths: a smooth bump centred on 538 nm, close to how sensitive the eye is, over 360 to 830 nm. umbra-spectrum uses it to pick each Pixel Sample's wavelengths (see the Camera Guide's sensor).

Each one also has an invert_…, which is simply the CDF: it says which u would have produced a given x.

Sampling a table

In plain wordsSometimes the density isn't a formula but a table of numbers: a pixel filter, or a picture of the sky. Then the crate builds the table's running total once, and inverts that. To sample, it finds which piece u falls in, then how far along that piece. In two dimensions it first picks a row, by how much each row holds in total, then a cell within that row.

Building a 1D distribution, as build_1d does: take absolute values (negative entries count by their size), add up the pieces' areas into the CDF, and divide by the total, the integral. If every value is zero, the CDF becomes a straight ramp, so sampling is uniform, and the pdf is reported as 0. Sampling uses find_interval from umbra-math, a binary search for the last CDF entry not above u:

let o = find_interval(cdf.len(), |index| cdf[index] <= u);
let mut du = u - cdf[o];
if cdf[o + 1] - cdf[o] > 0.0 {
    du /= cdf[o + 1] - cdf[o];                                 // how far along the piece
}
let pdf = if self.func_int > 0.0 { self.func[o] / self.func_int } else { 0.0 };
let x = lerp((float(o) + du) / float(self.func.len()), self.min, self.max);

The sample returns x, pdf and offset, the index of the piece. invert(x) runs it backwards. The 2D version keeps one conditional distribution per row and a marginal one over the row totals, and multiplies their pdfs. Its pdf(p) looks a point's cell up directly. The camera's pixel filter is sampled through one of these (Camera Guide, pixel filters). How the tables are stored without an allocator is section 16.

Points on a disk

In plain wordsA camera lens and a round lamp both need evenly spread points on a disk. The obvious way, distance from one number and angle from the other, needs a square root, or the points crowd the middle. Even with it, the square's cells become thin slivers on the disk. The concentric map turns each square ring into a circle ring instead, so cells stay compact and evenly spread numbers stay evenly spread.

Why the square root in the polar map? The area inside radius r grows as r². For the points to be even, the chance of landing inside radius r must also be r², so r² = u₀, which is the inversion method again. Without the root, half the points would land inside radius ½, a quarter of the area.

The concentric map, by Shirley and Chiu, first stretches the square to [−1, 1]². The larger of the two coordinates becomes the radius, and the ratio of the two becomes the angle within one of four wedges:

let u_offset = 2.0 * u - Vector2f::new(1.0, 1.0);
if u_offset.x == 0.0 && u_offset.y == 0.0 {
    return Point2f::new(0.0, 0.0);
}
let (r, theta) = if u_offset.x.abs() > u_offset.y.abs() {
    (u_offset.x, PI_OVER_4 * (u_offset.y / u_offset.x))
} else {
    (u_offset.y, PI_OVER_2 - PI_OVER_4 * (u_offset.x / u_offset.y))
};
let (sin, cos) = sin_cos(theta);

The concentric map is the one the camera's thin lens uses (Camera Guide), and the cosine-weighted hemisphere of the next section is built on it.

9umbra-sampling · warp/sphere.rs · sample_uniform_sphere, sample_uniform_hemisphere, sample_cosine_hemisphere, sample_uniform_cone · PBR 4e §A.5.2 to §A.5.4

Directions on a sphere

In plain wordsLight reaches a point from every direction. A direction is an arrow of length 1, which is a point on a ball of radius 1. So picking a direction means picking a point on that ball's surface, and “how likely” is counted per piece of that surface, called solid angle. The crate has four recipes: the whole sphere, the upper half, the upper half with more directions near the top, and a narrow cone.

The whole sphere. Archimedes noticed that slicing a sphere into slabs of equal thickness gives bands of equal area. So z can be picked evenly from −1 to 1, and the angle φ evenly around. The hemisphere is the same with z from 0 to 1. Hemispheres and cones in this crate always point along +z; callers turn them toward a surface normal with a coordinate frame.

Cosine-weighted. Light arriving at a grazing angle spreads over more of the surface, so it counts for less, by a factor cos θ (Lambert's law). Sampling directions in proportion to cos θ puts the work where it pays. Malley's method does it in one line: take a uniform point on the disk and lift it to the dome, z = safe_sqrt(1.0 - sqr(d.x) - sqr(d.y)).

A cone around +z of half-angle θmax, uniformly: pick cos θ evenly between cos θmax and 1. It is the shape a round light makes as seen from a point outside it.

Each comes with its density with respect to solid angle and an inverse. The cosine hemisphere's inverse is the concentric disk's inverse applied to (x, y).

10umbra-sampling · warp/triangle.rs · sample_uniform_triangle, sample_spherical_triangle · PBR 4e §A.5.5, §6.5.4

Points on a triangle

In plain wordsScenes are mostly triangles, and a lamp can be a triangle too. There are two ways to pick a point on one. By area: every bit of the triangle equally likely; simple and cheap. By solid angle: every bit equally likely as seen from the point being lit, so parts that look small from there get fewer samples. The second wastes fewer samples on lights that are close and seen at an angle.

A point on a triangle is written with barycentric coordinates (b₀, b₁, b₂): three weights that add up to 1, one per corner. The point is b₀v₀ + b₁v₁ + b₂v₂. Both functions return barycentrics as [f32; 3], and the shape code turns them into a point.

sample_uniform_triangle is Heitz's low-distortion map, which pbrt-v4 uses: whichever of u₀ and u₁ is smaller is halved, and the other gives the rest. It has no square roots, and it keeps nearby numbers on nearby points, so well-spread numbers stay well spread. The density with respect to area is 1 ⁄ (triangle area), which the shape computes. invert_uniform_triangle_sample goes back.

sample_spherical_triangle(v, p, u) is Arvo's method. It first picks how much of the curved triangle's area to cover, with u₀, which fixes a point c′ on one edge. Then it picks a direction along the arc from b to c′, with u₁. The direction is traced back to the flat triangle to get barycentrics. It returns a SphericalTriangleSample with the barycentrics and a pdf of 1 ⁄ A, A being the curved triangle's area. If the triangle is seen edge-on from p, the pdf is 0 and the sample is unusable. invert_spherical_triangle_sample recovers u from a direction.

No other crate calls these yet: the triangle Shapes and area Lights that need them come with later milestones.

Heitz's map. The square's two halves, either side of the diagonal, each fold onto the triangle. sample_uniform_triangle

11umbra-sampling · warp/bilinear.rs · sample_bilinear, bilinear_pdf · PBR 4e §A.5.6

The bilinear distribution

In plain wordsGive each corner of a square a weight, and blend the weights smoothly across it. Sampling that blend takes two straight-line choices: first the height y, using how heavy the bottom and top edges are; then the position x along that row, using how heavy its two ends are. Solid-angle triangle sampling uses it to favour the corners that face the light.
pub fn sample_bilinear(u: Point2f, w: [f32; 4]) -> Point2f {
    let y = sample_linear(u.y, w[0] + w[1], w[2] + w[3]);
    let x = sample_linear(u.x, lerp(y, w[0], w[2]), lerp(y, w[1], w[3]));
    Point2f::new(x, y)
}

This is the same marginal-then-conditional split as the 2D table, with straight lines instead of steps. bilinear_pdf gives 1 everywhere if all four weights are 0, and 0 outside the square. invert_bilinear_sample applies invert_linear_sample in the opposite order.

12umbra-sampling · warp/discrete.rs · sample_discrete, balance_heuristic, power_heuristic · PBR 4e §A.1, §2.2.3

Picking one of a few

In plain wordsSometimes the choice is between a handful of things: which of three lamps to sample, say, in proportion to how bright each is. sample_discrete lays the weights end to end on a ruler, drops u on it, and picks the piece it lands in. It also hands back where u landed inside that piece, rescaled to [0, 1), so the same number can be used again for the next choice. And when two different strategies could both have produced a sample, the MIS heuristics decide how much each one's estimate counts.

sample_discrete(weights, u) scans the weights from the left, adding them up until the running sum passes u × total. It returns None for an empty list, and the weights must add up to a positive number. If u × total rounds up to the total itself, it is nudged just below it with next_float_down. The result, a DiscreteSample, holds the index, its probability pmf, and u_remapped.

Multiple importance sampling (MIS). A renderer can reach the same light path in two ways: by sampling the BSDF, or by sampling the light. Each strategy is good where the other is bad. MIS uses both and weights each sample so the weights for any spot add up to 1, which keeps the sum unbiased. The balance heuristic weights by the share of the density; the power heuristic squares the densities first, which favours the stronger strategy more and usually gives less noise. nf and ng are how many samples each strategy takes.

13umbra-sampling · warp/henyey_greenstein.rs · henyey_greenstein, sample_henyey_greenstein · PBR 4e §11.3.1

Scattering in a medium

In plain wordsIn smoke, fog, milk or skin, light bounces off tiny particles. Which way it goes next is described by a phase function. The Henyey–Greenstein phase function has one knob, g. At 0 light scatters equally in every direction. Positive g mostly lets it carry on forward, like fog around a car's headlights. Negative g mostly sends it back. The crate evaluates it, and samples directions with exactly its shape.

Mind the directions. As everywhere in pbrt-v4, both point away from the particle: wo toward the viewer, wi toward where light comes from. Light that carries straight on has wi opposite to wo, so the cosine of the angle between them is −1. So forward scattering, g > 0, peaks at cos θ = −1, as the doc comment says.

The function's running total over cos θ can be inverted in closed form. sample_henyey_greenstein(wo, g, u) gets cos θ from u₀, the angle φ around wo from u₁, and turns the result into a direction with a coordinate frame built around wo (Frame::from_z in umbra-math). The pdf it returns is the phase function itself: this is perfect importance sampling, so f ⁄ p is always 1. g is clamped to [−0.99, 0.99], where the formulas stay accurate, and very small g is treated as 0. The layered BxDFs of a later milestone will use it.

Maths used here
p(cos θ) = (1 ⁄ 4π) · (1 − g²) ⁄ (1 + g² + 2g cos θ)3⁄2
The phase function, per steradian. It adds up to 1 over the sphere. henyey_greenstein(cos_theta, g)
cos θ = −(1 ⁄ 2g) · (1 + g² − ((1 − g²) ⁄ (1 + g − 2gu₀))²)

φ = 2πu₁,   wi = frame(wo) · (sin θ cos φ, sin θ sin φ, cos θ)

g = average of cos(angle of deflection)
What g means. The average cosine between the light's old and new travel directions: 0 for no preference, near 1 for barely deflected.
Part II · Implementation

How the crate is built

umbra-sampling is a core crate: #![no_std] without alloc, no f64, no trait objects, so the same code runs on the CPU and, later, on a GPU (ADR 0014, ADR 0015, ADR 0028). It depends on umbra-math only. Everything is re-exported at the crate root.

15umbra-sampling · sampler.rs · ADR 0006

Closed enums instead of trait objects

In plain wordspbrt-v4 has several samplers; Umbra has one so far. Rust could point at “some sampler” through the heap, but the core has no heap and a GPU can't follow such pointers. So every sampler is listed in one enum, held by value, and each call checks which one it holds. The same rule shapes the options: a choice is an enum with named variants, never a true/false.
pub enum SamplerKind<'a> {
    /// pbrt-v4 `"halton"`.
    Halton(HaltonSampler<'a>),
}

impl Sampler for SamplerKind<'_> {
    #[inline]
    fn get_1d(&mut self) -> f32 {
        match self {
            Self::Halton(s) => s.get_1d(),
        }
    }
    ...
}

Every match lists every variant and never uses _, so adding a sampler is a compile error at each place that must handle it (ADR 0006). There is one sampler per Render Job, so the branch is always predicted correctly. From<HaltonSampler> builds the enum: SamplerKind::from(HaltonSampler::new(...)).

A Scene names the randomisation with RandomizeStrategy, a plain enum whose default is PermuteDigits. The sampler holds a HaltonRandomizer, whose PermuteDigits variant carries the borrowed permutations. A sampler that permutes digits but has no permutations therefore can't be built: the type rules it out. pbrt-v4's fourth strategy, FastOwen, belongs to its Sobol samplers and arrives with them.

16umbra-sampling · piecewise.rs, lowdiscrepancy.rs · ADR 0008, ADR 0014

Tables without an allocator

In plain wordsA table's running total, and the Halton digit shuffles, need memory. The core crate can't allocate any. So the caller lends it: whoever builds the table passes a small function that returns a buffer of the size asked for. The Scene or the renderer, which can allocate, owns the memory. Per-Pixel-Sample code only borrows a cheap view of it.
pub fn new_in(f: &[f32], domain: Range<f32>, alloc: impl FnOnce(usize) -> S) -> Self
// once per Render Job, in a crate that can allocate (umbra-cpu)
let permutations = DigitPermutations::new_in(seed, |n| vec![0; n]);

// the sampler borrows them: a small value each thread copies
let randomizer = HaltonRandomizer::PermuteDigits(permutations.view());
let sampler = SamplerKind::from(HaltonSampler::new(spp, resolution, randomizer));

The structures are generic over their storage S: a Vec<f32> when owned, a &[f32] when borrowed. view() turns the first into the second without copying. new_in checks that alloc returned exactly the length asked for and panics otherwise, as it does for an empty table or an empty domain. A GPU backend can pass its own buffers through the same closure.

The digit permutations are the big one: about 26 MB for all 1000 bases, filled in a fraction of a second. pbrt-v4 computes them when it creates a HaltonSampler; Umbra does it once per Render Job, so a SamplerKind stays small and nothing is allocated per Pixel Sample (ADR 0008).

17umbra-sampling · README.md · ADR 0001, ADR 0004, ADR 0022

pbrt-v4's numbers, reproducibly

In plain wordsThe goal is that a seed draws pbrt-v4's numbers, and that a Pixel Sample computed twice gives the same bits. So the crate copies pbrt-v4's operations in pbrt-v4's order, in f32, and hashes the same bytes. Even the width of a hashed number matters: 37 as a 4-byte int and 37 as an 8-byte integer give different hashes, and therefore different sequences.
  • pbrt-v4's operations, in its order, in f32. No f64 anywhere in the crate (ADR 0001, ADR 0028): lib.rs turns on Clippy's disallowed_types lint, and clippy.toml lists f64. No implicit fused multiply-add: each multiplication and each addition is rounded on its own, in pbrt-v4's order. Each as conversion that may lose bits carries a written reason that names the C++ cast it copies, such as “as pbrt-v4: uint32_t(Hash(...)) * 0x1p-32f”.
  • Exact where possible. The hash, PCG32, the primes, the radical inverses and the Halton sampler use only integer operations and basic f32 arithmetic, so they give pbrt-v4's bits exactly. The warps call sin, atan2, exp and friends through umbra-math, which wraps glibc's C maths. They match pbrt-v4 up to the last-bit differences between that glibc and the maths library pbrt-v4 was built with.
  • pbrt-v4's comparisons. Where pbrt-v4 writes x < 0 || x > 1, so does Umbra, so a NaN takes the same branch. The comments say so where it matters.
  • Pure Pixel Samples. A Pixel Sample's numbers depend only on the Scene, the seed, the pixel and the sample index (ADR 0004, ADR 0022). start_pixel_sample rebuilds the sampler's state from scratch, and nothing carries over between Pixel Samples. That is what lets a Pixel Trace recompute one Pixel Sample later (GLOSSARY).
  • No accidental copies. Rng is Clone but not Copy: copying a generator silently would make two parts of the code draw the same numbers.
18umbra-sampling · tests/umbra_sampling · ADR 0013

Checked against pbrt-v4

In plain wordsTwo kinds of test. The first asks pbrt-v4 itself for answers (these hashes, these random numbers, these Halton points) and checks that Umbra gives the same bits. The second is statistical: draw a million samples from each warp and check that they really have the density the warp claims.
  • pbrt_v4/*_test.rs port pbrt-v4's own unit tests (hash_test.cpp, rng_test.cpp, sampling_test.cpp), each naming the TEST(Suite, Name) it ports. TEST(RNG, Double) is skipped: Umbra has no f64 samples.
  • known_values.rs holds values pbrt-v4 computes on fixed inputs, printed by known_values.cpp from pbrt-v4 at the pinned commit.
  • samplers_test.rs ports TEST(Sampler, ConsistentValues) and checks the primes, the radical inverses, the permutations and the Halton sampler under each randomisation, bit for bit, against halton_values.rs. This guide's JavaScript port passes the same tables.
  • sampling.rs runs the statistical tests on every warp and distribution, with fixed seeds. The χ² test in chi2.rs pools cells expected to get fewer than 5 samples, and its significance level is corrected for the 20 χ² tests in the suite, so a fair warp fails only rarely. The Jacobian is estimated by central differences.
  • generators.rs ports the sample generators pbrt-v4's tests draw from.

Run them with cargo nextest run -p umbra-sampling.
