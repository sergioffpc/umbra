Umbra Integrators Guide

A rendered image is an average of many small measurements, one per Pixel Sample. umbra-integrators is the crate that takes one Pixel Sample from start to end: it picks colours of light, asks the camera for a ray, asks the integrator how much light comes back along that ray, and hands the Film a colour and a weight. It also lets a recorder watch every step, so a single pixel can be inspected later. This guide explains each step in pictures first and formulas second. It assumes only school maths.

Part I · Theory

How a renderer measures light

A renderer answers one question millions of times: how much light arrives along this ray? This part explains the question, the physics behind it, and the five steps Umbra takes to answer it once.

0background · used by every section

The maths toolkit, in pictures

In plain wordsThis guide needs four ideas: estimating a total by averaging random guesses, carrying light as four colours at once, the word radiance for “how bright along this ray”, and a pure function that always gives the same answer for the same question. Vectors, angles, matrices and the filter are explained in the Camera Guide's toolkit and the Math Guide; this section does not repeat them.

Averages of random guesses (Monte Carlo)

Some totals are too hard to add up exactly, like all the light reaching a pixel. Monte Carlo estimates them: make a random guess, then another, and average. Each guess is noisy, but the average settles on the truth. A Pixel Sample is one such guess. Each guess is divided by how likely its random choice was, the probability density p, so that likely choices don't count twice. The Camera Guide has a longer picture of this.

total ≈ (1⁄N) Σ f(xi) ⁄ p(xi)

Light as four wavelengths

Light is a mix of colours, one amount for every wavelength λ (lambda) from violet, about 360 nm, to deep red, about 830 nm. Umbra never stores RGB while light travels. Each Pixel Sample picks four wavelengths at random and tracks the light at just those four: a SampledWavelengths and a SampledSpectrum, each four numbers. Over many Pixel Samples every colour gets its turn. The Spectrum Guide explains these types.

L = (L(λ₁), L(λ₂), L(λ₃), L(λ₄))

Radiance: how bright, along one ray

Radiance L(p, ω) is how much light arrives at a point p from one direction ω, per unit of area and per unit of angle. Think of looking through a very thin straw: radiance is what you see. In empty space it stays the same all along the straw. That is why a renderer can follow a ray backwards from the camera and ask what is at the other end. The integrator's answer, RayIntegrator::li, is a radiance.

L(p, ω): watts per square metre per steradian, at each wavelength

A pure function

A pure function depends only on its inputs: no clock, no hidden counter, no randomness of its own. Umbra makes every Pixel Sample a pure function of the Scene, the seed, the pixel and the sample index (ADR 0022). The “random” numbers come from a sampler that is restarted from those inputs, so they are the same each time. This is what lets a Pixel Sample be computed again later to see how it was made.

The rendering equation, in plain words

In plain wordsThe light leaving any point toward your eye is two things added together. First, the light the point gives off itself, if it glows. Second, all the light arriving at it from every direction, times how much the surface bounces each of those toward you. The catch: the light arriving from each direction is itself light leaving some other point, which follows the same rule. Light bounces around. That is the whole difficulty of rendering.

Each piece in words:

  • Lo(p, ωo) is the light leaving point p in direction ωo. This is what the camera ray wants to know.
  • Le is light the point makes itself: a lamp, the sun's disc, a glowing sphere. Most points emit nothing.
  • The ∫ adds up over every direction ωi in the half-sphere H² above the surface.
  • f(p, ωo, ωi), the BSDF, says what fraction of light arriving from ωi leaves toward ωo. A matte wall spreads light evenly; a mirror sends it all one way.
  • cos θ accounts for slant: light arriving at a grazing angle is spread over more area, like low winter sun, so it lights each bit of surface less.

On the camera ray itself nothing happens until it hits something. If it hits nothing at all, it “escapes” and sees only lights at infinity, such as a sky. In Umbra today every camera ray escapes and there is no sky, which is why every image is black (section 11). The full equation, with media such as fog added, is what VolPathIntegrator will estimate once Shapes and lights arrive (#79, #80).

How do you estimate the ∫? Exactly like any other Monte Carlo total: pick a random direction, measure f · Li · cos θ there, and divide by how likely that direction was. The figure below does this at one point, in a flat 2D world so it fits on a screen.

Maths used here
Lo(p, ωo) = Le(p, ωo) + ∫H² f(p, ωo, ωi) Li(p, ωi) |cos θi| dωi
The rendering equation (pbrt calls it the light transport equation). Light leaving = light made + light bounced. Not in the code yet: RayIntegrator::li will estimate it once intersections and shading arrive (#79, #80).
Li(p, ω) = Lo(first point hit from p along ω, −ω)
Why it is recursive. The light arriving from a direction is the light leaving whatever you see in that direction. With nothing in the way, it is the light of the lights at infinity, or 0.
Lo ≈ Le + f(ω′) Li(ω′) cos θ′ ⁄ p(ω′)
One Monte Carlo guess. Pick one direction ω′ at random with density p, measure there, divide by p. Averaged over many Pixel Samples this gives the true value.
flatland: Lo = ∫−π⁄2π⁄2 (ρ⁄2) Li(θ) cos θ dθ
The figure's 2D version for a matte surface of brightness ρ (rho, the albedo, between 0 and 1). The ρ⁄2 makes a white wall under uniform light 1 reflect exactly ρ, because ∫ cos θ dθ = 2.
uniform: p(θ) = 1⁄π   ·   cosine: p(θ) = cos θ ⁄ 2, θ = asin(2u − 1)
Two ways to pick a direction. Uniform picks every angle equally. Cosine-weighted picks steep angles more often, matching the cos θ in the integral, so the guesses vary less. This is importance sampling, as in the Sampling Guide.

What an integrator is

In plain wordsAn integrator is the part of a renderer that answers “how much light comes back along this ray?”. It gets a ray and the four wavelengths, and returns four numbers: the radiance at each wavelength. How it finds the answer is its strategy. Different integrators chase light in different ways, but they all answer the same question. The name comes from the ∫ of the rendering equation: an integrator estimates that integral.

The trait, as it is in pixel_sample.rs:

pub trait RayIntegrator {
    fn li<R: Recorder>(
        &self,
        view: &SceneView<'_>,
        ray: &Ray,
        lambda: &mut SampledWavelengths,
        state: &mut PixelSampleState<'_, R>,
    ) -> SampledSpectrum;
}

The name li is pbrt-v4's Li: “L incoming”, the radiance arriving at the ray's origin. A ray integrator does not decide which rays to shoot; evaluate_pixel_sample does that and calls li once per Pixel Sample. Splitting it this way lets every ray integrator share the same front half: wavelengths, camera sample, camera ray, and the same back half: sensor RGB, film sample.

The R: Recorder parameter is what makes Pixel Traces possible (section 4), and why li is generic rather than callable through a trait object (section 10).

Maths used here
L(λ1..4) ≈ Li(o, −d) at λ1..4
What li estimates. The radiance arriving at the ray's origin o, travelling against its direction d, at each of the four wavelengths. One call gives one noisy guess; the Film's average over Pixel Samples removes the noise. RayIntegrator::li
E[ guess ] = true value
Unbiased. The average (E, the “expected value”) of a good integrator's guesses is the exact answer, so more Pixel Samples always get closer. A path cut short at max_depth bounces is the one known exception: it leaves out light that needs more bounces.

Evaluating one Pixel Sample

In plain wordsA Pixel Sample is named by two numbers: which pixel, and which sample of that pixel (the 0th, the 1st, …). From that name alone, evaluate_pixel_sample runs five steps. Restart the random numbers for this name. Pick four colours of light. Pick a spot in the pixel and make a ray through it. Ask the integrator how much light comes back. Turn that light into a colour and hand it to the Film with a weight. Same name in, same answer out, every time.

The function, complete, from pixel_sample.rs:

pub fn evaluate_pixel_sample<I: RayIntegrator, R: Recorder>(
    integrator: &I,
    view: &SceneView<'_>,
    id: PixelSampleId,
    state: &mut PixelSampleState<'_, R>,
) -> FilmSample {
    state.sampler.start_pixel_sample(id.pixel, id.sample_index, 0);       // restart
    let mut lambda = SampledWavelengths::sample_visible(state.sampler.get_1d());  // ①
    state.recorder.wavelengths(&lambda);

    let camera_sample = get_camera_sample(&mut state.sampler, id.pixel, view.filter()); // ②
    state.recorder.camera_sample(&camera_sample);

    let l = match view.camera().generate_ray(camera_sample, &lambda) {         // ③
        Some(camera_ray) => {
            state.recorder.camera_ray(&camera_ray);
            camera_ray.weight * integrator.li(view, &camera_ray.ray, &mut lambda, state) // ④
        }
        None => SampledSpectrum::splat(0.0),
    };
    state.recorder.radiance(&l);

    let sample = FilmSample {                                                   // ⑤
        rgb: view.sensor().to_sensor_rgb(l, &lambda),
        weight: camera_sample.filter_weight,
    };
    state.recorder.film_sample(&sample);
    sample
}

Three things to notice. The sampler restarts first. start_pixel_sample rebuilds the sampler's state from the pixel and the sample index, starting at dimension 0, so nothing carries over from the previous Pixel Sample. Callers never call it themselves. The wavelengths go to the sensor after li. lambda is passed to li as &mut, so the sensor sees any change the integrator made. Nothing is written anywhere. The result is returned as a FilmSample, and the caller adds it to its pixel (section 9).

Compared with pbrt-v4's RayIntegrator::EvaluatePixelSample, three things are missing on purpose, each waiting for the feature that needs it: ray differentials (which the camera does not make yet), the check that turns a NaN or infinite radiance into zero (Anomalous Pixel Samples, #85), and the visible-surface record of pbrt-v4's G-buffer film.

The recorder and Pixel Traces

In plain wordsSometimes one pixel looks wrong, and you want to see exactly how it was computed. Umbra doesn't save that during a render; that would be slow and huge. Instead it computes the same Pixel Sample again later, with a recorder attached that writes down every step. Because a Pixel Sample is a pure function, the second run is the same as the first. While rendering, the recorder is NoRecorder: it writes nothing, takes no memory, and the compiler removes it entirely.

The trait and the recorder used for rendering, from recorder.rs (one method shown of five):

pub trait Recorder {
    fn wavelengths(&mut self, lambda: &SampledWavelengths);
    fn camera_sample(&mut self, sample: &CameraSample);
    fn camera_ray(&mut self, ray: &CameraRay);
    fn radiance(&mut self, l: &SampledSpectrum);
    fn film_sample(&mut self, sample: &FilmSample);
}

pub struct NoRecorder;

impl Recorder for NoRecorder {
    #[inline(always)]
    fn wavelengths(&mut self, _: &SampledWavelengths) {}
    // … the other four, all empty and #[inline(always)]
}

// Rendering pays nothing for the Recorder seam (ADR 0011).
const _: () = assert!(size_of::<NoRecorder>() == 0, "`NoRecorder` is zero-sized");

Why it costs nothing: evaluate_pixel_sample is generic over R, so the compiler makes a separate copy of it for each recorder type (monomorphisation). In the NoRecorder copy, every call is to an empty function marked #[inline(always)], so it vanishes, and a zero-sized value takes no space in PixelSampleState. The const assertion stops the build if someone ever gives NoRecorder a field. ADR 0011 rejected the alternatives: a Cargo feature (the trace binary would differ from the rendering one), a runtime if (not free on the hottest path), and a dyn Recorder (no trait objects on the per-ray path, ADR 0006).

The point of a Pixel Trace is trust: it shows what was rendered, not what a separate debugging path thinks was rendered. It is produced by umbra trace, which re-runs chosen Pixel Samples in one local process with the same Scene, seed and Render Job options. That is exact on the machine and binary that rendered them (ADR 0022). The same tool will inspect Anomalous Pixel Samples, those whose radiance is NaN, infinite or negative, which the Film will leave out and report (#85).

The scene view

In plain wordsA Pixel Sample needs to read parts of the Scene: the camera, the filter, the sensor, and later the shapes and lights. Instead of handing it the whole Scene, Umbra hands it a view: a small bundle of borrowed references to exactly those parts. Borrowing means nothing is copied, and many threads can read the same view at once. The GPU will build the same kind of view over its own copies of the data.

The whole type, from view.rs:

pub struct SceneView<'a> {
    camera: &'a CameraKind,
    filter: FilterKind<&'a [f32]>,
    sensor: &'a PixelSensor<'a>,
}

impl<'a> SceneView<'a> {   // signatures only; each body is one line
    pub fn new(camera: &'a CameraKind, filter: FilterKind<&'a [f32]>, sensor: &'a PixelSensor<'a>) -> Self
    pub fn camera(&self) -> &'a CameraKind
    pub fn filter(&self) -> &FilterKind<&'a [f32]>
    pub fn sensor(&self) -> &'a PixelSensor<'a>
}

umbra-scene's Scene::view builds it with SceneView::new(&self.camera, self.filter.view(), &self.sensor). Why not just pass &Scene? Three reasons from the architecture. Layering: umbra-scene depends on this crate, not the other way round, so this crate cannot even name Scene (ADR 0015). No heap in the core: the view holds only borrows and slices, never a Vec, so it fits a no_std crate without alloc (ADR 0014). One core for CPU and GPU: the GPU will build a SceneView over device copies of the same arrays, and the same per-sample code runs on it (ADR 0028). Shapes, lights and the BVH will join the view as more borrowed arena slices, with the features that use them.

The fields are private and read through methods, so they can change shape later without touching callers. Building a view is cheap: umbra-render builds one per Work Unit and shares it between the threads that render it.

Path tracing: the theory behind volpath

In plain wordsPath tracing estimates the rendering equation by following one light path at a time, backwards from the camera. The ray hits a surface. If the surface glows, add its glow. Then pick one random direction to bounce, and keep a running factor of how much light survives: the throughput. Repeat until the path escapes, hits the bounce limit, or is cut short by a coin toss. A volumetric path tracer also lets the path stop in mid-air inside fog or smoke and scatter there. Umbra's VolPathIntegrator is named for this, but today it does none of it yet: the Scene has nothing to hit.

The structure of pbrt-v4's VolPathIntegrator::Li, in words, is a loop over bounces. Each turn: intersect the ray with the Scene; if it misses, add the light of the infinite lights and stop; if the hit point glows, add β · Le; if depth has reached max_depth, stop; sample a light and add its direct contribution; sample the BSDF for a new direction and multiply β by f cos θ ⁄ p; then possibly stop by Russian roulette. Media add a step before all this: sample a distance along the ray in the medium, and scatter there if it comes before the surface.

Russian roulette ends paths that carry little light without biasing the answer. When β gets small, the path survives only with probability 1 − q; if it survives, β is divided by 1 − q to make up for the paths that were stopped. On average, nothing is lost. max_depth is the hard limit: light that needs more bounces than that is lost, which is the one bias the integrator accepts. pbrt-v4's default, and Umbra's, is 5.

The figure below runs a simplified version of this loop in a flat 2D room, next to what Umbra's VolPathIntegrator does today.

Maths used here
L ≈ Σk βk · Le(xk)
One path's guess. Add the glow of each vertex xk, dimmed by the throughput βk of the bounces before it. Not in the code yet: VolPathIntegrator::li returns 0 today.
βk+1 = βk · f(xk) · |cos θk| ⁄ p(ωk)
Throughput update at each bounce: the Monte Carlo guess of the rendering equation's ∫, one direction at a time. For a matte surface with cosine-weighted directions this simplifies to β × albedo.
q = max(0, 1 − maxλ β),   survive with 1 − q, then β ← β ⁄ (1 − q)
Russian roulette as pbrt-v4 applies it, after the first bounce (depth > 1), when the largest component of β is below 1. The sampler provides the coin toss, so a path stays a pure function of its Pixel Sample.
stop when depth = max_depth (default 5)
The bounce limit. With 0 you only see lights directly; with 1, light that bounced once; and so on. VolPathIntegrator::new(max_depth), max_depth() — stored today, not used yet.
T(d) = e−σt d
Transmittance through a uniform medium with extinction coefficient σt (sigma t, how thick the fog is): the fraction of light that gets through a distance d. Not in Umbra yet.

How the crate is built

umbra-integrators is the top of Umbra's rendering core: #![no_std] without alloc, no f64, no trait objects on the per-ray path, so the same code can later run on a GPU (ADR 0014, ADR 0015, ADR 0028). It depends on umbra-camera, umbra-shading, umbra-spectrum, umbra-sampling, umbra-geometry and umbra-math; umbra-scene and umbra-render depend on it. It covers PBR 4e chapters 13 to 15, and today implements the frame those chapters hang on.

One function for rendering and tracing

In plain wordsThere is exactly one function that computes a Pixel Sample, and everybody calls it: the renderer, and later the trace tool. It starts by resetting the random numbers from the Pixel Sample's name. So it always does the same thing for the same name, no matter which thread, in which order, or how many times.

evaluate_pixel_sample is pbrt-v4's EvaluatePixelSample with StartPixelSample moved inside. pbrt-v4 leaves the restart to its caller; Umbra puts it inside, so no caller can forget it or call it differently. The README states the rule for users: callers do not call start_pixel_sample themselves.

This is the foundation of two ADRs. ADR 0004 (as amended by ADR 0022) makes a Pixel Sample a pure function of (Scene, seed, pixel, sample index) on one machine, and has each pixel sum its Pixel Samples in ascending sample index. ADR 0011 then gets Pixel Traces for free: compute the same Pixel Sample again with a recording R. Both callers use the same compiled code for every step except the recorder's calls.

Maths used here
FilmSample = E(view, seed, id)
A pure function. The seed reaches E through the sampler's randomizer, the Scene through the view, the id directly. Nothing else goes in. evaluate_pixel_sample
pixel sum = Σi = first … last FilmSample(pixel, i), in ascending i
A fixed order of additions. Floating-point addition gives different last bits in a different order, so the order is fixed (ADR 0022). Done by umbra-render's pixel loop, which folds the sample indices in order.

Values in, values out

In plain wordsEvery step takes values and returns values. The only things that change during a Pixel Sample, the sampler and the recorder, are bundled into one PixelSampleState that a thread owns and lends to each Pixel Sample in turn. The result comes back as a small FilmSample. Nothing here knows about Tiles, threads or the Film. That keeps it allocation-free, and lets the GPU call the very same steps later.
pub struct PixelSampleState<'s, R> {
    pub sampler: SamplerKind<'s>,   // restarted at each Pixel Sample
    pub recorder: R,                 // NoRecorder when rendering
}

pub struct FilmSample {
    pub rgb: RGB,       // the radiance in the sensor's RGB
    pub weight: f32,    // the filter weight of the camera sample
}

And the caller, abridged from umbra-render's PixelLoop::render_pixel, which folds a pixel's sample indices in ascending order:

samples.clone().fold(PixelSum::default(), |mut sum, sample_index| {
    let id = PixelSampleId { pixel, sample_index };
    sum += evaluate_pixel_sample(&self.0, &job.view, id, state);
    sum
})

Three rules meet here. No allocation per Pixel Sample (ADR 0008): the state is created once and passed by &mut; the counting-allocator test checks it. No f64 in the core (ADR 0028, enforced by the clippy::disallowed_types lint in lib.rs): the FilmSample is f32, and the caller widens it before adding, because sums over many samples need the extra precision (ADR 0001). Steps as functions on values (ADR 0028): a GPU wavefront can run the same steps from its own queues.

Maths used here
sumc += f64(w · rgbc),   sumw += f64(w)
How a FilmSample is added by the caller: multiply in f32, then add in f64. PixelSum: AddAssign<FilmSample> in umbra-render
f32: about 7 significant digits; f64: about 16
Why sum in f64. Adding a small number to a big f32 total loses the small number's last digits. After thousands of samples that adds up; f64 keeps it negligible.
10umbra-integrators · pixel_sample.rs · ADR 0006

Generic, not dyn

In plain wordsRust can call a method in two ways. Static: the compiler knows the exact type and can paste the code inline. Dynamic (dyn): the type is looked up at run time through a table of function pointers. Dynamic calls are a little slower and can't be inlined, and the GPU can't follow them. So the per-ray code is static. The one dynamic call in rendering happens once per Work Unit, outside this crate, where its cost doesn't matter.

A Rust trait with a generic method, like fn li<R: Recorder>, cannot be made into a trait object: there would be one method per R, too many to put in a table. So RayIntegrator is not object-safe, on purpose. ADR 0006 bans trait objects on the per-ray path anyway, and asks for closed enums where several kinds exist (as CameraKind and SamplerKind do). With one integrator so far, there is no enum of integrators yet; umbra-render wraps the concrete integrator in a generic PixelLoop<I> and calls it through Box<dyn Integrator> once per Work Unit, which ADR 0006 allows because it is not per ray.

Maths used here
copies compiled = (integrators) × (recorders)
The cost of generics is code size, not speed: today 1 × 1 (VolPathIntegrator × NoRecorder) in the renderer, and 1 × 2 once TraceRecorder exists. evaluate_pixel_sample::<I, R>
dynamic calls per Render Job = Work Units, not Pixel Samples
Where the dyn sits. A 1920 × 1080 image in 32 × 32 Tiles, 4 Passes: 60 · 34 · 4 = 8 160 dynamic calls, against 1920 · 1080 · spp static ones.
11umbra-integrators · volpath.rs · VolPathIntegrator · PBR 4e §14.2

VolPathIntegrator today

In plain wordsHonest status: the volumetric path tracer is a placeholder. The Scene has no Shapes and no lights yet, so every camera ray flies off into nothing, and nothing out there glows. The right answer to “how much light comes back?” is therefore zero, and that is what it returns, without even drawing a random number. It already stores max_depth, the bounce limit, ready for when paths can bounce.

All of volpath.rs's logic:

pub struct VolPathIntegrator {
    max_depth: u32,
}

impl VolPathIntegrator {
    pub const fn new(max_depth: u32) -> Self { Self { max_depth } }
    pub const fn max_depth(self) -> u32 { self.max_depth }
}

impl Default for VolPathIntegrator {
    fn default() -> Self { Self::new(5) }   // pbrt-v4's "maxdepth" default
}

impl RayIntegrator for VolPathIntegrator {
    // The ray escapes, and there is no infinite light to add:
    // zero radiance, with no sample drawn.
    fn li<R: Recorder>(&self, _view: &SceneView<'_>, _ray: &Ray,
                        _lambda: &mut SampledWavelengths,
                        _state: &mut PixelSampleState<'_, R>) -> SampledSpectrum {
        SampledSpectrum::splat(0.0)
    }
}

Every argument is unused, which the leading underscores say. “No sample drawn” matters for later: once li reads numbers from the sampler, they will follow the camera sample's, in pbrt-v4's order. The type also has no media support by design for v0.1.0, which refuses Scenes that have them; the name keeps pbrt-v4's so a Scene File's Integrator "volpath" maps onto it. umbra-scene holds a VolPathIntegrator in its Scene and hands it to the renderer.

What stays true when it grows: the signature. Intersections will read Shapes and the BVH through the SceneView, random choices will come from state.sampler, and every vertex, light sample and BSDF sample will be reported to state.recorder.

Maths used here
Li(ray) = Σinfinite lights Le = 0
An escaping ray sees only lights at infinity. There are none, so the sum is empty and the radiance is 0 at every wavelength. SampledSpectrum::splat(0.0)
rgb = k · ¼ Σ c̄(λ) · 0 ⁄ p(λ) = (0, 0, 0),   weight ≈ 0.94
What the Film receives today: black, with a normal weight, so the pixel's value Σ w·rgb ⁄ Σ w is exactly 0. FilmSample

How it is tested

In plain wordsThe crate has no test files of its own yet. It is tested from the outside, through the renderer that calls it: the image must be black, must not depend on how many threads rendered it, and rendering must not allocate memory per Pixel Sample. The README's example also runs as a test.
  • umbra-render/tests/umbra_render/renderer.rs: a_camera_only_scene_renders_black checks every pixel's RGB sum is 0; work_results_do_not_depend_on_the_thread_count compares 1 and 4 threads; a_render_is_a_pure_function_of_its_work_unit renders every Work Unit again, in reverse order, with a new Renderer, and compares. All go through evaluate_pixel_sample and VolPathIntegrator.
  • umbra/tests/allocation.rs: rendering_allocates_per_work_unit_not_per_pixel_sample counts heap blocks with dhat for 4 and then 8 Pixel Samples per pixel and expects the same count, so nothing is allocated per Pixel Sample (ADR 0008). Every integrator will join it.
  • The README's example builds the camera, filter and sensor by hand, computes Pixel Sample (3, 4) #2 with NoRecorder, and asserts the RGB is (0, 0, 0) and the weight is above 0. It runs as a doctest.
  • At compile time: size_of::<NoRecorder>() == 0.

Run them with cargo nextest run -p umbra-render -p umbra and cargo test --doc -p umbra-integrators. Tests that compare radiance with pbrt-v4 will come with the first Shapes and lights; the Reference Scenes are checked by eye (ADR 0031).

Maths used here
blocks(8 spp) = blocks(4 spp)
The allocation test's idea. If anything allocated per Pixel Sample, doubling them would raise the count. Equal counts mean all allocation is per Work Unit.
13umbra-integrators · roadmap

Not here yet

In plain wordsThe frame is built; the engine inside it comes next. Here is what is missing, and which issue brings it.
  • Intersections, emission and shading in VolPathIntegrator::li, with #79 (emissive spheres visible to the camera) and #80 (diffuse surfaces lit by area lights). Shapes, lights and the BVH join SceneView as arena slices, and the path loop of section 6 takes shape: BSDF sampling, light sampling, MIS, Russian roulette, max_depth.
  • Media (fog, smoke, the “vol” in volpath): v0.1.0 refuses Scenes that have them.
  • Leaving out Anomalous Pixel Samples, with #85. Until then a NaN or infinite radiance reaches the Film, where pbrt-v4 would zero it.
  • TraceRecorder and text Pixel Traces through umbra trace, with #88. It lives in umbra-render, not here.
  • More recorder events: path vertices, light and BSDF samples, Russian roulette, termination, BVH node visits and primitive tests (ADR 0011), added with the code that makes them.
  • Ray differentials and pbrt-v4's scaling of them by the number of Pixel Samples, once textures need them.
  • Other integrators: BDPT, SPPM, MLT, with CPU drivers of their own (arc42 §8.12), and the GPU's wavefront over the same steps in umbra-kernels.
