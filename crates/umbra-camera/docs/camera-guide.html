Umbra Camera Guide

How does a renderer decide what each pixel shows? It shoots rays out of a virtual camera, one per Pixel Sample, and measures the light that comes back along them. umbra-camera handles both ends of that trip. This guide explains each step in pictures first and formulas second. It assumes only school maths.

  1. umbra-samplingSamplergives numbers in [0, 1)
  2. umbra-cameraget_camera_samplenumbers → where on film and lens
  3. umbra-cameraCameraSamplep_film, p_lens, time, filter_weight
  4. umbra-cameragenerate_rayfilm point → ray
  5. umbra-integratorsL(λ)light found along the ray
  6. umbra-camerato_sensor_rgblight → sensor RGB
  7. umbra-renderFilmweighted average per pixel

How to read this guide. Each section starts with a blue In plain words box and a diagram. Then comes the detail, with a Maths used here box listing every formula next to the Rust function that computes it. Last comes an interactive figure: move the sliders and watch the result change. New to the symbols? Start with section 0. The figures repeat the Rust code's operations in JavaScript (in double precision, so the last digits can differ from Umbra's). Section references (“PBR 4e §5.2”) point to Physically Based Rendering, 4th edition, which Umbra follows. The matrix maths of perspective() is covered in more depth in the Umbra Math Field Guide.

Part I · Theory

How a camera sees

For every Pixel Sample a camera answers two questions. Which ray does it follow into the scene? And how much does the light coming back along that ray count in the image?

0background · used by every section

The maths toolkit, in pictures

In plain wordsThe whole guide uses only five ideas: arrows with a length, angles turned into heights, boxes of numbers that move points, areas under curves, and averages. Each one has a picture below. If a formula later looks scary, come back here.

Points, vectors, length

A point is a place, (x, y, z). A vector is an arrow: a direction and a length. A ray is a starting point plus a direction vector. Only the direction matters there, so the code shrinks the arrow to length 1. That is normalising.

|v| = √(x² + y² + z²)   normalize(v) = v ⁄ |v|

Angles become heights: tan

Look straight ahead and raise your eyes by an angle θ. At distance 1 you are looking at height tan θ. That is how the camera's field of view becomes the size of the image: half the view angle gives half the image height.

height at distance 1 = tan θ

Matrices move points

A 4 × 4 matrix is a recipe that moves every point the same way: stretch it (scale), slide it (translate), turn it, or squash it with distance (perspective). Recipes chain by multiplying, read right to left, and the inverse undoes a recipe. The camera builds the recipe “camera → pixels” and then inverts it to go from pixels back to the camera.

scale(a, b, c): (x, y, z) ↦ (a·x, b·y, c·z)
translate(t): p ↦ p + t

Areas under curves, estimated by averaging

The integral ∫ f(x) dx is the area under a curve. Rendering is full of such areas, and none can be computed exactly. Monte Carlo estimates the area by measuring the height at random spots and averaging. More spots give a better estimate. Importance sampling picks spots more often where the curve is tall. To stay fair, each measurement is divided by how likely its spot was, the probability density p(x). If p has exactly the shape of f, every measurement f ⁄ p gives the same number, the true area. This trick drives the pixel filter and the sensor.

∫ f(x) dx ≈ (1⁄N) Σ f(xi) ⁄ p(xi)

1umbra-camera · PBR 4e §5.1, §5.4, §8.8

What a pixel measures

In plain wordsA pixel is a small light bucket, not a dot. Light reaches it from slightly different spots on the film, through different parts of the lens, while the shutter is open, in every colour of the rainbow. The renderer can't add all of that up exactly. Instead it takes many random Pixel Samples, gives each one a weight, and averages them.

The full integral for one channel of pixel (x, y) has one ∫ for each thing that varies: film position, lens position, time and wavelength. f is the pixel filter and r̄ the sensor's colour response:

I(x, y) = ∫film ∫lens ∫time ∫λ f(p − (x, y)) · r̄(λ) · L(p, plens, t, λ) dλ dt dplens dp

Monte Carlo replaces each ∫ with “pick a random value, divide by how likely it was”. The result splits neatly into the two halves of the crate:

one Pixel Sample = f(p) ⁄ pf(p) × r̄(λ) L(λ) ⁄ pλ(λ)
  • The first factor is the filter_weight of a CameraSample.
  • The second factor is what PixelSensor::to_sensor_rgb returns.
  • Between the two, Camera::generate_ray decides which light L gets measured, by turning the film and lens points into a ray.

From a pixel to a ray: the pinhole

In plain wordsPicture a window in front of your eye, divided into a grid of pixels. To see what one pixel shows, draw a line from your eye through that pixel and keep going. That line is the ray. Most of the code converts “a position in pixels” into “a position on that window”, in a few small steps.

The film position p_film arrives in raster coordinates, counted in pixels from the top-left corner with y going down. The ray must leave the camera in camera space: the eye at (0, 0, 0) looking along +z, with +y up and +x to the right. In between are two helper spaces. The diagram shows the whole chain:

  • Screen space is the image plane. The screen window covers it: the shorter side of the image spans −1 to 1, and the longer side is stretched by the aspect ratio. A 16 × 9 image gets [−16⁄9, 16⁄9] × [−1, 1].
  • NDC (normalised device coordinates) squeezes that window into 0 to 1, starting at its top-left corner.
  • Raster multiplies NDC by the number of pixels and flips y to point down.

PerspectiveCamera::new writes that chain from the camera's side, in the same operations as pbrt-v4, and keeps only the inverse, which runs from the pixel back to the camera:

let screen_from_camera = perspective(fov, 1e-2, 1000.0);
let ndc_from_screen = scale(1.0 / (p_max.x - p_min.x), 1.0 / (p_max.y - p_min.y), 1.0)
    * translate(Vector3f::new(-p_min.x, -p_max.y, 0.0));
let raster_from_ndc = scale(resolution.x, -resolution.y, 1.0);
let raster_from_screen = raster_from_ndc * ndc_from_screen;
let camera_from_raster = screen_from_camera.inverse() * raster_from_screen.inverse();

Making a pinhole ray then takes one matrix application and one normalisation. Every ray starts at the eye:

let p_camera = self.camera_from_raster.apply(Point3f::new(p_film.x, p_film.y, 0.0));
let ray = Ray::new(Point3f::new(0.0, 0.0, 0.0), Vector3f::from(p_camera).normalize());
Maths used here
xndc = (xs − pmin,x) ⁄ (pmax,x − pmin,x)
yndc = (pmax,y − ys) ⁄ (pmax,y − pmin,y)
Screen → NDC. Slide the window's top-left corner to 0 and divide by the window's size. The y is measured downward from the top. ndc_from_screen
xr = W · xndc,   yr = H · yndc
NDC → raster. Multiply by the image's width W and height H in pixels. raster_from_ndc
xs = x ⁄ (z · tan(fov⁄2)),   ys = y ⁄ (z · tan(fov⁄2))
Perspective. Divide by depth z, so things twice as far look half as big. Then divide by tan(fov⁄2), so the edge of the view lands at ±1. perspective(fov, n, f) in umbra-math
zs = f (z − n) ⁄ (z (f − n))
Depth. The same matrix also maps depths between the near plane n = 0.01 and the far plane f = 1000 onto 0 to 1. A ray tracer barely uses this: raster points sit at zs = 0, which lands on the near plane.
d = pcamera ⁄ |pcamera|
The direction. The arrow from the eye to the point, shrunk to length 1. Vector3f::normalize
3umbra-camera · camera.rs · ThinLens · uses umbra-sampling sample_uniform_disk_concentric · PBR 4e §5.2.3

The thin lens and depth of field

In plain wordsA pinhole keeps everything sharp. A real lens is a disc: light passes through all of it and is bent to meet again at one distance, the focal distance. Things at that distance are sharp, and things nearer or farther are blurred. To copy this, each Pixel Sample starts its ray at a random point on the lens disc. The ray still passes through the same point on the plane of focus.

In code, a radius above 0 turns the pinhole ray into a lens ray:

if self.lens.radius > 0.0 {
    let p_lens = sample_uniform_disk_concentric(sample.p_lens) * self.lens.radius; // ②
    let ft = self.lens.focal_distance / ray.d.z;                                 // ①
    let p_focus = ray.at(ft);
    ray.o = Point3f::new(p_lens.x, p_lens.y, 0.0);                                 // ③
    ray.d = (p_focus - ray.o).normalize();
}

The ray's weight stays 1. The lens point is drawn evenly over the disc, so averaging the samples already averages over the lens. Umbra's ThinLens holds only a radius and a focal distance. A radius of 0 gives a pinhole, which is pbrt-v4's default.

Maths used here
t = df ⁄ dz,   pfocus = t · d
Where the pinhole ray meets the plane of focus. The ray climbs dz in depth per unit of length, so it needs t units to reach depth df. ray.at(ft)
a = 2u − 1,   b = 2v − 1
if |a| > |b|: ρ = a, φ = (π⁄4)(b⁄a)
else: ρ = b, φ = π⁄2 − (π⁄4)(a⁄b)
p = (ρ cos φ, ρ sin φ)
Concentric map, square → disc. Centre the square on 0. The bigger coordinate gives the distance from the centre ρ, and the ratio of the two gives the angle φ. sample_uniform_disk_concentric in umbra-sampling
o = (r · px, r · py, 0),   d′ = normalize(pfocus − o)
The lens ray. It starts on the lens and aims at the focus point.
width(z) = 2r · |1 − z ⁄ df|
blur angle ≈ 2r · |1⁄z − 1⁄df|
How blurred is depth z? The width of the cone of rays at that depth, and the angle it covers as seen from the camera. The angle is 0 at the plane of focus and grows with the lens radius. The figure divides it by one pixel's angle to give the blur in pixels.
4umbra-camera · filter.rs · GaussianFilter, FilterSampler · PBR 4e §8.8

Pixel filters

In plain wordsThe filter decides how much a sample counts for a pixel, by how far it is from the pixel's centre. Samples near the centre count a lot, samples farther out count less, and past the radius they don't count. Umbra doesn't compute that weight afterwards. It places samples where the filter is high more often, so every sample ends up with the same weight and belongs to just one pixel.

Umbra's Gaussian is pbrt-v4's: the familiar bell curve, lowered by its own value at the radius so that it reaches exactly zero there, and zero beyond. In two dimensions it is the bell in x times the bell in y.

To place samples in proportion to the filter, you need the inverse of its running total, and the Gaussian's running total has no simple formula. So FilterSampler tabulates the filter on a grid, then samples the table. The diagram shows the idea in one dimension:

Maths used here
gσ(x) = e−x² ⁄ (2σ²) ⁄ √(2πσ²)
The bell curve (Gaussian) of width σ. gaussian(x, 0, sigma) in umbra-math
f(x, y) = max(0, gσ(x) − gσ(r)) · max(0, gσ(y) − gσ(r))
Umbra's filter. The bell lowered to touch zero at the radius r. The defaults are r = 1.5 pixels, σ = 0.5. GaussianFilter::evaluate
∫f = (erf(r ⁄ (σ√2)) − 2r · gσ(r))²
The filter's total area. erf is the bell curve's running total, a standard function. The lowering removes a strip of height gσ(r) and width 2r. Squared, because the filter is x times y. integral(), via gaussian_integral
n = int(32 · r),   centrei = −r + 2r · (i + ½) ⁄ n
The table. n × n cells across [−r, r]², each holding the filter at its centre. 48 × 48 for the defaults. table_size, cell_centre
p(cell) = fcell ⁄ (Σ f · cell area)
w = fcell ⁄ p(cell) = Σ f · cell area
The weight is always the same. Its density matches the table exactly, so f ⁄ p is always the table's area. That is very close to ∫f, and a constant factor cancels in the Film's Σ w·c ⁄ Σ w. FilterSampler::sample
5umbra-camera · camera.rs · get_camera_sample, CameraSample · PBR 4e §5.1.1, §8.8.4

The camera sample

In plain wordsThe sampler is like a tape of random numbers between 0 and 1. get_camera_sample takes five numbers off the tape, always in the same order. Two become the offset inside the pixel, via the filter. One becomes the time. Two become the point on the lens. The result is a CameraSample, everything the camera needs to make a ray.
let fs = filter.sample(sampler.get_pixel_2d());   // u₁ u₂: offset from the filter
CameraSample {
    p_film: Point2f::from(pixel) + fs.p + Vector2f::new(0.5, 0.5),
    time: sampler.get_1d(),                         // u₃
    p_lens: sampler.get_2d(),                       // u₄ u₅
    filter_weight: fs.weight,
}

The time is drawn even though the camera doesn't move yet. Skipping it would shift every number after it on the tape, and the rays would no longer match pbrt-v4's.

Maths used here
pfilm = (x + ½ + δx, y + ½ + δy)
Film position. Pixel (x, y) covers the square from x to x + 1, so its centre is at + ½. Add the filter's offset δ, which can reach up to r in any direction.
t = (1 − u) · topen + u · tclose
Time in the shutter interval. A straight-line blend from the opening time to the closing time. Camera::sample_time, via lerp
6umbra-camera · sensor.rs · PixelSensor · uses umbra-spectrum SampledWavelengths · PBR 4e §4.6.3, §5.4.2

The pixel sensor: light to colour

In plain wordsUmbra tracks light the way physics does: as an amount at each wavelength, not as RGB. A camera sensor has three kinds of cells, each sensitive to a different range of wavelengths. Each cell multiplies the light by its sensitivity at every wavelength and adds it all up. Umbra checks only four random wavelengths per Pixel Sample and divides by how likely each one was. Over many samples, the average comes out right.
let l = l.safe_div(lambda.pdf());                               // divide by how likely each λ was
let response = |bar: &DenselySampledSpectrum<'_>| (bar.sample(lambda) * l).average();
RGB::new(self.imaging_ratio * response(&self.r_bar), ...)

The four wavelengths come from SampledWavelengths::sample_visible. Wavelengths near 538 nm, where the eye is most sensitive, are picked most often, and the four are spread a quarter apart so they don't bunch up. The default sensor, PixelSensor::cie1931, uses the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions x̄, ȳ, z̄ as its three curves. Its “RGB” is therefore the standard XYZ colour space. Real camera sensors and white balance come later (#84).

Maths used here
X = k ∫ x̄(λ) L(λ) dλ ≈ k · ¼ Σi=1..4 x̄(λi) L(λi) ⁄ p(λi)
One channel. The area under the product, estimated from 4 wavelengths. k is the imaging ratio, exposure time × ISO ⁄ 100, 1 by default. to_sensor_rgb
a ⁄ b if b ≠ 0, else 0
Safe division. A wavelength with density 0 contributes nothing instead of producing a NaN. SampledSpectrum::safe_div
λ(u) = 538 − 138.888889 · atanh(0.85691062 − 1.82750197 u)
Picking a wavelength. Turns a random u into a wavelength between 360 and 830 nm, most often near 538. This is the inversion method from section 4, with a formula instead of a table. sample_visible_wavelengths in umbra-spectrum
p(λ) = 0.0039398042 ⁄ cosh²(0.0072 (λ − 538))
How likely each wavelength is. A bump centred on 538 nm. cosh is a U-shaped curve, so 1 ⁄ cosh² is a bell-like bump.
ui = u + i⁄4 (minus 1 if over 1),   i = 0..3
Four spread-out wavelengths from one random number. SampledWavelengths::sample_visible
Part II · Implementation

How the crate is built

umbra-camera is a core crate: #![no_std] without alloc, no f64, no trait objects, so the same code can later run on a GPU. It sits above umbra-geometry and umbra-spectrum, and umbra-integrators depends on it (ADR 0014, ADR 0015).

Two rules shape the crate. Only what making a ray needs. PerspectiveCamera keeps camera_from_raster, the lens and the base parameters. pbrt-v4's extra members arrive with the features that use them. No Film in the core. pbrt-v4's camera holds its Film. Umbra's camera takes only the resolution, and the Film lives in umbra-render.

8umbra-camera · ADR 0006

Closed enums instead of trait objects

In plain wordsThere will be several kinds of camera. Rust could point at “some camera” through a pointer to the heap, but the core has no heap and a GPU can't follow such pointers. So Umbra lists every kind in one enum and keeps the camera inline. Each call checks which kind it holds and runs that code.
pub enum CameraKind {
    Perspective(PerspectiveCamera),
}

impl Camera for CameraKind {
    #[inline]
    fn generate_ray(&self, sample: CameraSample, lambda: &SampledWavelengths) -> Option<CameraRay> {
        match self {
            Self::Perspective(c) => c.generate_ray(sample, lambda),
        }
    }
    ...
}

With one variant today, the match compiles away entirely. When a new camera arrives it becomes one more variant, and the compiler then flags every match that doesn't handle it yet. Each concrete type converts into its enum with From, so PerspectiveCamera::new(...).into() gives a CameraKind. FilterKind<S> works the same way.

9umbra-camera · filter.rs · ADR 0014, ADR 0008

A filter table without an allocator

In plain wordsThe filter's table needs memory, but the core crate can't allocate any. So the caller lends it the memory: whoever builds the filter passes a small function that produces storage of the requested size. The Scene can allocate, so it owns the table. The rendering loop only borrows a cheap view of it.
pub fn new_in(
    radius: Vector2f,
    evaluate: impl Fn(Point2f) -> f32,
    mut alloc: impl FnMut(usize) -> S,   // called for the table, then the distribution
) -> Self
// built once, in umbra-scene
let filter: FilterKind<Vec<f32>> =
    GaussianFilter::new_in(Vector2f::new(1.5, 1.5), 0.5, |n| vec![0.0; n]).into();

// per Pixel Sample, allocation-free
let sample = get_camera_sample(&mut sampler, pixel, &filter.view());

new_in checks that the closure returned storage of the right length. It also checks that the radius is at least 1⁄32, since a smaller radius would give a table with no cells. A GPU backend can pass its own buffers through the same closure. PixelSensor<'a> solves the same problem with a lifetime: it borrows its curves, which are static for CIE 1931.

10umbra-camera · camera.rs · CameraTransform · PBR 4e §5.1.1

Camera-world rendering space

In plain wordsComputer numbers (f32) are very precise near zero and get coarser the bigger they are. The things you see best are near the camera. So Umbra slides the whole world until the camera sits at zero, and the important geometry gets the most precise numbers. It slides the world but doesn't turn it.
pub fn new(world_from_camera: &Transform) -> Self {
    let p_camera = world_from_camera.apply(Point3f::new(0.0, 0.0, 0.0));      // where the camera is
    let world_from_render = translate(Vector3f::from(p_camera));               // slide only
    let render_from_camera = world_from_render.inverse() * *world_from_camera; // keeps the rotation
    Self { render_from_camera, world_from_render }
}

The rotation stays in render_from_camera. The ray is built in camera space and then transformed once, by that matrix. Note the argument: world_from_camera is the inverse of the current transformation at a Scene File's Camera directive. The camera doesn't move while the shutter is open (yet), so both transforms are fixed.

Maths used here
ULP(x) = 2⌊log₂|x|⌋ − 23
The gap between neighbouring f32 numbers near x. It doubles every time x doubles. Near 1 the gap is about 0.00000012. Near 10 000 it is about 0.001. f32 has 23 fraction bits, hence the 23.
11umbra-camera · tests/umbra_camera · ADR 0013

Checked against pbrt-v4

In plain wordsTo prove Umbra's camera behaves like pbrt-v4's, a small C++ program asks pbrt-v4 itself for answers: these rays, these filter values, this sensor colour. The Rust tests compare against them.
  • known_values.cpp links against a pbrt-v4 build at the pinned commit and prints the reference values. known_values.rs holds them.
  • cameras.rs checks pinhole and thin-lens rays, and get_camera_sample with the Halton sampler. The latter checks the draw order too.
  • filters_test.rs ports pbrt-v4's own filter tests (zero past the radius, correct integral) and checks evaluate, integral and sample.
  • sensor.rs checks the default sensor's RGB.

The comparisons allow a few ULPs of difference. pbrt-v4's compiler may fuse a multiply and an add into one step, and its sin and exp come from a different maths library. A ray is compared in ULPs of its largest component, because a component near zero can come out of a subtraction of nearly equal numbers and carry a large relative error. Run the tests with cargo nextest run -p umbra-camera.

12umbra-camera · roadmap

Not here yet

  • Other cameras: orthographic, spherical (360°) and realistic (real lens systems). Each becomes a new CameraKind variant.
  • Other filters: box, triangle, Mitchell, Lanczos. Some have negative parts, which give samples negative weights. pbrt-v4 samples by the size of f and keeps its sign in the weight.
  • Importance functions (We, PDF_We, SampleWi), for integrators that trace paths from the lights back to the camera.
  • Ray differentials: the neighbouring pixels' rays, used to blur textures by the right amount.
  • Moving cameras (motion blur), and the other rendering spaces.
  • Named camera sensors, white balance and the other RGBFilm settings, with #84.
