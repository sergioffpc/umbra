Umbra Spectrum Guide

What colour is a red apple? To a renderer, the honest answer is not three numbers. It is a curve: how much light the apple sends back at every wavelength of the rainbow. umbra-spectrum is how Umbra stores those curves, carries four points of them through each Pixel Sample, and turns them back into the RGB of an image. This guide explains each step in pictures first and formulas second. It assumes only school maths.

  3. umbra-spectrumSpectrumKinda whole curve over 360–830 nm
  4. umbra-spectrumsample_visible4 wavelengths and their PDFs
  5. umbra-spectrumSampledSpectrumthe curve at those 4 points
  6. umbra-spectrumto_xyz4 values → XYZ
  7. umbra-spectrumto_rgbXYZ → linear RGB

How to read this guide. Each section starts with a blue In plain words box and a diagram. Then comes the detail, with a Maths used here box listing every formula next to the Rust function that computes it. Last comes an interactive figure: move the sliders and watch the result change. New to the symbols? Start with section 0. The figures repeat the Rust code's operations in JavaScript, in double precision, using pbrt-v4's CIE 1931 and D65 tables at 5 nm (the ones its RGB-to-spectrum optimiser uses). Umbra's run-time tables are at 1 nm, so the last digits can differ. Section references (“PBR 4e §4.6”) point to Physically Based Rendering, 4th edition, which Umbra follows. How a camera sensor uses these spectra is in the Umbra Camera Guide, and the Vec4 lanes and matrices come from the Umbra Math Guide.

Part I · Theory

How light becomes colour

Light carries energy at many wavelengths at once. The eye boils that down to three numbers. A renderer has to keep the many while it simulates light, and produce the three when it writes the image.

The maths toolkit, in pictures

In plain wordsThis crate needs only four ideas: a curve that gives one number per wavelength, “multiply two curves and add up the result”, a 3 × 3 box of numbers that mixes three numbers into three others, and an S-shaped curve that squeezes any number into the range 0 to 1. Each one has a picture below. Averages and probability densities (Monte Carlo) are explained in the camera guide's toolkit, and matrices in more depth in the math guide.

A curve over wavelength: a spectrum

The horizontal axis is the wavelength λ (lambda) in nanometres (nm), from violet at 360 to deep red at 830. The height is “how much”. Ask the curve about one wavelength and it answers with one number. That is all a Spectrum does: eval(λ) returns the height at λ.

s(λ): wavelength in, one number out

Multiply, then add up

To see how strongly a curve f “excites” another curve g, multiply their heights at each wavelength and add all the products. The result is the shaded area. Mathematicians call it the inner product. Umbra computes it with one product per whole nanometre, 471 of them.

⟨f, g⟩ = Σλ = 360..830 f(λ) · g(λ)

A 3 × 3 matrix mixes three numbers

A colour is three numbers. A matrix with 3 rows and 3 columns turns three numbers into three new ones: each output is a weighted mix of all three inputs, with one row of weights per output. The inverse matrix M−1 undoes the mix. Colour spaces are pairs of such matrices.

outi = mi1·in1 + mi2·in2 + mi3·in3

The sigmoid squeezes into 0 to 1

A sigmoid is an S-shaped curve. Feed it any number x, as large or as negative as you like, and it answers with a number between 0 and 1: very negative gives almost 0, 0 gives ½, very positive gives almost 1. A surface can't reflect more than all the light or less than none, so this is the right shape for a reflectance.

s(x) = ½ + x ⁄ (2√(1 + x²))

Light is a spectrum

In plain wordsLight is a mix of waves of many lengths. Our eyes see waves from about 380 to 750 nanometres, and we see each length as a colour of the rainbow. Any light, from the sun or a bulb, has some amount of each. Plot “how much” against “which wavelength” and you get a curve: the light's spectrum. Surfaces have curves too: the fraction of light they send back at each wavelength.

In code, a spectrum is anything that answers three questions. The Spectrum trait has exactly these three methods:

pub trait Spectrum {
    fn eval(&self, lambda: f32) -> f32;                          // the height at one wavelength
    fn max_value(&self) -> f32;                                // a bound on the height everywhere
    fn sample(&self, lambda: &SampledWavelengths) -> SampledSpectrum; // the heights at 4 wavelengths
}

Umbra has five kinds of spectrum so far. Two are simple:

  • ConstantSpectrum has the same height c at every wavelength. A grey surface that reflects 50 % of everything is ConstantSpectrum::new(0.5).
  • DenselySampledSpectrum is a table with one value per whole nanometre, starting at lambda_min. Asking for 512.4 nm rounds to the nearest whole nanometre, 512, and reads that entry. Off the end of the table the answer is 0. The CIE curves and D65 are stored this way.

The other three turn an RGB into a curve, and get their own section 6.

Blackbodies: light from heat

Heat anything enough and it glows. A blackbody is an ideal glowing object whose spectrum depends only on its temperature T, in kelvin (K). Planck's law gives the curve. At 2700 K (a warm light bulb) most of the energy is infrared, beyond the red end. At 5500 K the peak sits in the middle of the visible range and the glow looks white. Hotter still, the peak moves into blue. Wien's law says where the peak is. pbrt-v4 describes many lamps by a temperature, and divides the curve by its peak so the largest value is 1. Umbra doesn't have BlackbodySpectrum yet (see section 12), but the figures below use the same formula.

Why render spectrally

In plain wordsWhen light hits a surface, each wavelength is multiplied by the surface's reflectance at that same wavelength. A cheaper renderer multiplies three RGB numbers instead. That works for smooth, ordinary spectra, but it goes wrong when the light or the surface has peaks and gaps. Two paints can look identical in daylight and different under a street lamp. RGB can't tell them apart; a spectrum can. So Umbra simulates light only as spectra, never as RGB.

Each bounce multiplies wavelength by wavelength. For one wavelength the arithmetic is just “light × reflectance”. The question is what to do with all the wavelengths at once:

  • Spectral: multiply the curves, point by point, and only at the very end ask the eye's three questions (section 4).
  • RGB: ask the three questions first, then multiply three numbers. The order is swapped, and “add up, then multiply” is not the same as “multiply, then add up”.

ADR 0001 settles it: radiance in Umbra is always a SampledSpectrum, there is no RGB rendering mode, and RGB appears only at the edges. Scene Files give RGB colours, and the image is written in RGB. This crate holds both edges: RGB → spectrum (section 6) and spectrum → RGB (sections 4 and 5).

3umbra-spectrum · sampled.rs · SampledWavelengths, SampledSpectrum · uses umbra-sampling sample_visible_wavelengths · PBR 4e §4.5.4, §5.4.2

Four wavelengths per Pixel Sample

In plain wordsA whole curve is too much to carry through millions of light paths. So each Pixel Sample picks just four wavelengths at random and follows the light at those four only. Different Pixel Samples pick different wavelengths, and their average covers the whole rainbow. The pick favours the middle of the rainbow, where the eye is most sensitive, and remembers how likely each pick was so the average stays fair.

N_SPECTRUM_SAMPLES is 4, fixed (ADR 0001): four f32 fill one 128-bit SIMD register exactly. SampledWavelengths offers two ways to pick them:

  • sample_uniform(u, range) puts the first at u of the way along range, then steps by a quarter of the range, wrapping round once. Every wavelength gets the same density, 1 ⁄ (width of the range). The test uniform_wavelengths_wrap_around checks that u = 0.5 over 400..800 gives 600, 700, 800, 500.
  • sample_visible(u) is what a Pixel Sample uses. It feeds u, u + ¼, u + ½, u + ¾ (wrapped) to sample_visible_wavelengths from umbra-sampling, and stores each one's density from visible_wavelengths_pdf.

A wavelength that is picked often must count for less, or the bright middle would be over-counted. That is why every estimate divides by pdf(). safe_div makes the division safe: where the density is 0 (a terminated lane), the answer is 0 instead of “not a number”.

pub fn sample_visible(u: f32) -> Self {
    let lambda = [0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0]
        .map(|i: f32| sample_visible_wavelengths(wrap(u + i / N_SAMPLES, 0.0, 1.0)));
    Self {
        lambda: Vec4::from(lambda),
        pdf: SampledSpectrum::from(lambda.map(visible_wavelengths_pdf)),
    }
}

A SampledSpectrum supports what pbrt-v4's does: + − × ÷ lane by lane, average, min_component_value, max_component_value, is_nonzero (true if any lane isn't 0), clamp, sqrt, exp, pow, lerp and bilerp. Any spectrum fills one with Spectrum::sample(&lambda).

How the eye sees: XYZ

In plain wordsThe eye doesn't measure a whole spectrum. It has three kinds of colour cells, and each reports one number. In 1931 scientists measured how people match colours and wrote down three curves, x̄, ȳ and z̄. Multiply a spectrum by each curve and add up: you get three numbers, X, Y and Z. Two lights with the same X, Y, Z look the same, however different their spectra. Y alone is how bright the light looks.

There are two ways to compute XYZ in the crate, for two situations:

  • Exactly, for a whole spectrum: spectrum_to_xyz(s) takes the inner product of s with each matching function, one term per nanometre from 360 to 830, in order. It divides by CIE_Y_INTEGRAL = 106.856895, the area under ȳ, so that a constant spectrum of 1 has Y = 1. Colour spaces use this once, when they are built.
  • Estimated, from four wavelengths: SampledSpectrum::to_xyz(&lambda) is what a Pixel Sample can afford. It multiplies the four values by the matching functions at the four wavelengths, divides each by its density, and averages. One Pixel Sample's answer is noisy; the average of many is right. The camera guide's sensor figure shows this estimate homing in. y(&lambda) computes the Y part alone.
pub fn to_xyz(self, lambda: &SampledWavelengths) -> XYZ {
    let pdf = lambda.pdf();
    let estimate = |cmf: SampledSpectrum| (cmf * self).safe_div(pdf).average();
    XYZ::new(
        estimate(spectra::X.sample(lambda)),
        estimate(spectra::Y.sample(lambda)),
        estimate(spectra::Z.sample(lambda)),
    ) / CIE_Y_INTEGRAL
}

spectrum_to_photometric is a close cousin: just the inner product with ȳ, how bright a light looks, used to set a light's power from a brightness. For an RGBIlluminantSpectrum it measures the colour space's illuminant alone and ignores the colour. XYZ::xy gives the chromaticity, and XYZ::from_xy_y goes back from a chromaticity and a brightness Y.

RGB colour spaces

In plain wordsA screen makes colours by mixing three lights: a red, a green and a blue. Which red, green and blue, exactly, and which mix counts as “white”, is what a colour space pins down. Given those four facts, a little algebra yields a 3 × 3 matrix that turns any RGB into XYZ, and its inverse, which turns XYZ back into RGB. sRGB, the colour space of most screens and images, is the only one in Umbra so far.

RGBColorSpace keeps the three primaries r, g, b and the white w as chromaticities, its illuminant as a StdIlluminant, the two matrices, and a reference to its RGB-to-spectrum table (section 6). The constructor is private; RGBColorSpace::srgb() calls it with the Rec. 709 primaries (0.64, 0.33), (0.3, 0.6), (0.15, 0.06) and D65 white:

let white = spectrum_to_xyz(illuminant.spectrum());
let [pr, pg, pb] = [r, g, b].map(|c| XYZ::from_xy_y(c, 1.0));
let rgb = SquareMatrix::new([[pr.x, pg.x, pb.x], [pr.y, pg.y, pb.y], [pr.z, pg.z, pb.z]]);
let c = rgb.inverse()? * <[f32; 3]>::from(white);
let xyz_from_rgb = rgb * SquareMatrix::diag(c);
// ... rgb_from_xyz: xyz_from_rgb.inverse()?

The ? makes the constructor return None if the primaries lie on one line, where pbrt-v4 exits; for sRGB that can't happen, so srgb() unwraps. Then:

  • to_xyz(rgb) multiplies by xyz_from_rgb, to_rgb(xyz) by rgb_from_xyz. These are linear RGB values, proportional to light; the gamma curve of an image file is applied later, by whoever writes it.
  • luminance_vector() is the middle row of xyz_from_rgb: the weights that turn RGB into brightness Y. For sRGB, green weighs most, about 0.715.
  • A colour outside the triangle, like a pure 520 nm green, gets a negative R or B. The maths is fine with it; a screen can't show it.

Build it once. srgb() computes the white point and inverts two matrices on every call. pbrt-v4 builds sRGB once at start-up; callers in Umbra build it once and keep it.

6umbra-spectrum · color.rs, rgb_to_spectrum.rs, spectrum.rs · RGBSigmoidPolynomial, to_rgb_coeffs, RGBAlbedoSpectrum, RGBUnboundedSpectrum, RGBIlluminantSpectrum · PBR 4e §4.6.6

From RGB back to a spectrum

In plain wordsScene Files describe colours as RGB, but Umbra needs curves. Infinitely many curves have the same RGB, so the crate picks a well-behaved one: smooth, and between 0 and 1. The trick is a curve with only three knobs, a parabola squeezed by a sigmoid. For each RGB, the three knob settings were found ahead of time by a search, and stored in a big table. At render time, finding the curve is a quick table lookup.

The curve is an RGBSigmoidPolynomial: three f32, c0, c1, c2, and eval(λ) = sigmoid(c0λ² + c1λ + c2) with λ in nm. Because the sigmoid only rises, the curve is highest where the parabola is: at 360, at 830, or at the parabola's turning point −c1 ⁄ (2c0) if that lies between them. max_value checks exactly those three places. This method (Jakob and Hanika, 2019) is what pbrt-v4 uses.

Finding the coefficients: ahead of time

For every grid point, a search called Gauss–Newton adjusts the three coefficients until the curve, lit by D65, has the target RGB. It measures the error in CIELAB, a colour space where equal distances look equally different. The search for one grid point starts from the answer of its neighbour, walking up and down the z axis from z node 12. Umbra's build script (section 9) ports pbrt-v4's rgb2spec_opt for this, operation by operation.

Looking up: at render time

RGBColorSpace::to_rgb_coeffs(rgb) clamps negatives to 0 and asks the table. A grey (R = G = B) skips the table: a flat curve at height r needs c0 = c1 = 0 and sigmoid(c2) = r, which has an exact answer. Otherwise the lookup works out maxc, z, x, y, finds the cell, and blends its corners along x, then y, then z, in pbrt-v4's order.

Three RGB spectra, by what the RGB means

  • RGBAlbedoSpectrum: a reflectance or transmittance, each channel in [0, 1]. The curve itself. Debug builds check the bound.
  • RGBUnboundedSpectrum: a value that may exceed 1, such as a scattering coefficient. The RGB is divided by twice its largest channel (so the largest becomes ½), looked up, and the curve multiplied back by that scale.
  • RGBIlluminantSpectrum: a light's colour. Like the unbounded one, then multiplied by the colour space's illuminant, D65 for sRGB. Without that, an RGB of (1, 1, 1) would mean a flat spectrum, which looks slightly warm next to daylight; with it, (1, 1, 1) means “the space's white”.
fn scaled_coeffs(cs: &RGBColorSpace, rgb: RGB) -> (f32, RGBSigmoidPolynomial) {
    let m = max(rgb.r, max(rgb.g, rgb.b));
    let scale = 2.0 * m;
    let rgb = if scale == 0.0 { RGB::default() } else { rgb / scale };
    (scale, cs.to_rgb_coeffs(rgb))
}

Part II · Implementation

How the crate is built

umbra-spectrum is a core crate: #![no_std] without alloc, no f64 at run time, no trait objects, so the same code can run on a GPU (ADR 0014, ADR 0028). It sits on umbra-math (the Vec4 lanes, matrices) and umbra-sampling (the visible-wavelength density), and umbra-shading, umbra-camera and umbra-integrators build on it.

One closed enum for every spectrum

In plain wordsA material might hold any of the five spectrum kinds. Instead of pointing at “some spectrum” on the heap, Umbra lists all five in one enum, SpectrumKind, and stores it by value. Each method asks which kind it holds and runs that kind's code. A GPU can do this; it can't follow a pointer to a function.
impl Spectrum for SpectrumKind<'_> {
    fn eval(&self, lambda: f32) -> f32 {
        match self {
            Self::Constant(s) => s.eval(lambda),
            Self::DenselySampled(s) => s.eval(lambda),
            Self::RGBAlbedo(s) => s.eval(lambda),
            Self::RGBUnbounded(s) => s.eval(lambda),
            Self::RGBIlluminant(s) => s.eval(lambda),
        }
    }
    ...
}

Following ADR 0006, the interface is a trait with the book's name, Spectrum; each concrete type keeps its pbrt-v4 name and implements it; the enum that gathers them is SpectrumKind; and every match is written out by hand and lists every variant, never _. Adding a sixth spectrum is then a compile error everywhere it must be handled. spectrum_to_photometric shows the rule at work: its special case for illuminants lists the other four variants by name.

Each concrete type converts with From, so RGBAlbedoSpectrum::new(&srgb, rgb).into() gives a SpectrumKind. DenselySampledSpectrum can't own its values: the core has no allocator (ADR 0014). It borrows a slice, which is why the enum carries 'a. For the built-in curves that slice is a static, so 'a is 'static.

9umbra-spectrum · spectra.rs, lib.rs, build/main.rs, build/rgb2spec.rs · ADR 0014, ADR 0022, ADR 0028

Data built before the program runs

In plain wordspbrt-v4 fills its colour tables when the program starts. Umbra does that work earlier. The CIE curves and D65 are computed by the compiler and baked into the program as fixed data. The big RGB-to-spectrum table is computed by a build script, a small program Cargo runs before compiling the crate, and included as raw bytes. At run time nothing is set up: the tables are just there, the same on every run.

Rounding like C++. pbrt-v4 writes its tables as decimal literals in float arrays, and C++ reads each as a double first, then rounds it to float. Umbra keeps the literals as f64 in spectra/data.rs and rounds them with narrow, a const fn that does v as f32. Rounding twice through a double can differ from reading the decimal straight into f32, so this is what keeps the values pbrt-v4's to the bit. The f64 exists only during compilation: #![warn(clippy::disallowed_types)] forbids it at run time (ADR 0028), and each compile-time use carries an #[expect] with its reason.

D65 at luminance 1. pbrt-v4 stores D65 as (wavelength, value) pairs every 5 nm. densely_sample_normalized repeats, in a const fn, what pbrt-v4 does at start-up: interpolate the pairs at each whole nanometre (piecewise_linear, with pbrt-v4's FindInterval bisection), and scale so that Σ D65 · ȳ = CIE_Y_INTEGRAL.

A name instead of a reference. A colour space and an RGBIlluminantSpectrum refer to their illuminant by StdIlluminant::D65, a 4-byte Copy enum, and look the spectrum up with spectrum(). A Scene arena that holds one then holds no pointer, so it can be copied to a GPU or another machine as plain bytes (ADR 0014).

The table, generated. 3 × 64³ cells × 3 coefficients is about 2.36 million f32, too big to commit. build/rgb2spec.rs ports pbrt-v4's rgb2spec_opt operation for operation in f64; build/main.rs writes the result to OUT_DIR; RGBToSpectrumTable::from_le_bytes, a const fn, checks the length at compile time and splits the bytes into the z nodes and the coefficients. The values are read with f32::from_le_bytes when looked up.

Determinism. The table is a pure function of its sources (ADR 0022): IEEE f64, which Rust never fuses into multiply-adds, the hardware square root, and glibc's cbrt; each cell is the same whichever thread computes it. Its last bits can still differ from pbrt-v4's own table (pbrt-v4's build lets the C++ compiler fuse operations) and with the glibc it is built against, so no test pins them: the tests allow a relative error of 10⁻⁶ wherever the table is involved.

10umbra-spectrum · sampled.rs · uses umbra-math Vec4 · ADR 0001, ADR 0022

Four lanes of f32, in pbrt-v4's order

In plain wordsA processor can add four numbers to four others in one step, if they sit side by side in one register. Four wavelengths fit that exactly, so SampledSpectrum is one Vec4. There is a catch. Computer numbers round after every step, so adding the same four numbers in a different order can give a different answer. Umbra adds them in the same order as pbrt-v4, so the answers match to the last bit.

The crate's test reductions shows why the order is pinned: the average of 10⁸, 1, −10⁸, 1 in f32 is 0.25 when summed in order, though the exact answer is 0.5. Near 10⁸, f32 numbers are 8 apart, so adding 1 to 10⁸ changes nothing. A different order would give a different wrong answer, and a different image in the last bits.

Two operations don't promise pbrt-v4's bits: exp and pow use umbra-math's four-lane kernels, which are within 1 ULP (one step between neighbouring f32 values) of the correctly rounded result rather than the bits of glibc's expf and powf (ADR 0022). fast_exp maps umbra-math's scalar fast_exp over the lanes. NaN handling follows pbrt-v4 too: is_nonzero counts NaN as non-zero, and clamp_zero turns NaN into 0, as C++ std::max(0, v) does.

Every type stored in a Scene arena is #[repr(C)], and compile-time assertions pin its size and field offsets: RGB, XYZ and RGBSigmoidPolynomial are 12 bytes, StdIlluminant 4. SampledSpectrum lives in registers, not arenas; it is #[repr(transparent)] over its Vec4.

11umbra-spectrum · tests/umbra_spectrum · ADR 0013

Checked against pbrt-v4

In plain wordsTo prove Umbra's spectra behave like pbrt-v4's, a small C++ program asks pbrt-v4 itself for answers on fixed inputs: these wavelengths, these RGB spectra, this colour space. The Rust tests compare against them, bit for bit where the arithmetic is the same.
  • spectrum_test.rs ports pbrt-v4's spectrum_test.cpp: each matching function integrates to about CIE_Y_INTEGRAL; a constant 1 has XYZ about (1, 1, 1); every spectrum stays under its max_value; importance-sampling ȳ with the visible density gives its integral within 0.1 %. Then known values: X, Y, Z and D65 at all 471 wavelengths, bit for bit; sample_visible and sample_uniform; to_xyz, y and to_rgb.
  • color_test.rs ports color_test.cpp, the sRGB cases: RGB → XYZ → RGB returns the same RGB; the XYZ-to-RGB matrix's columns; D65 comes out as RGB (1, 1, 1); the RGB spectra attain their max_value and round-trip their RGB within 0.01. Then known values: sRGB's chromaticities and both matrices bit for bit, and 43 RGB colours through each of the three RGB spectra.
  • sampled.rs checks SampledSpectrum and SampledWavelengths on worked examples: lane-wise arithmetic, NaN and −0 in is_nonzero, safe_div and the clamps, the order of reductions, the uniform wrap, and terminate_secondary.
  • generators.rs ports the sample generators pbrt-v4's tests draw from, so the ported tests see the same inputs.

Run them with cargo nextest run -p umbra-spectrum.
